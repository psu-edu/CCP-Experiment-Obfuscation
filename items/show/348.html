For the Colored American. A CONVENTION. Mr. Editor, -- As the public mind has been d directed to the subject of a general convention of the " "people of color" by yourself and your numerous correspondents, it may not be amiss for one that has acted the part of a member in all our former assemblies of like character, to express an opinion respecting the proposed object. I have observed that your correspondents have been less definite respecting the subjects that should claim the attention of the convention, when assembled, than they are of the fact that the convention ought to be held. Those that are friendly to a convention being held, ought first, if they would influence the public mind in favor of such a measure, state distinctly the subjects that ought in their opinion to claim the attention of such an assemblage when convened. For curiosity, powerful as it often acts upon the popular mind, will not produce an impulse strong enough to make men leave their homes, and business to travel hundreds of miles at a great expense, merely to hear others reiterate the views they have already conceived. Let the objects be fully marked out, and then they will meet to execute them, not merely to contemplate what ought to be done. I am not an advocate for a general convention of the " "people of color," (in their representative character) although I am deeply impressed with the necessity of such an assemblage. We need it much in order that we might concentrate our common energies, both as a means of defence against our common foes - and to aid in effecting our own improvement. But how can this be achieved under present circumstances? ? The minds of our people have been directed to no one common object, for they have been idly observing the great moral movements of the age, and the fluttering waves of expediency. In popular assemblies the representative follows the views of his constituents, and the delegates to such a convention when assembled, if true to the trust reposed in them, would represent the same divided sentiment that exists in the heterogeneous mass of mind that exists throughout the country. The very question whether we should abide in our native land, and "bear the ills we have," or seek in distant shores the privileges we

[PAGE 2]
are here deprived of, would divide the convention asunder. My opinion is that we have nothing to expect from the action of a general convention of the "people of color." All large assemblies are impatient, and almost uncontrolable, and in this country popular assemblies unless they are under the power of the civil law are unmanageable. If the whole American people were now assembled in a delegated character, with all their learning, wisdom and piety, it would be difficult for them to adopt a constitution for their government. Reforms are pushed forward now, not by the whole people, in their delegated character but by the friends of certain measures, meeting together for the purpose of carrying forward their own cherished objects. If we expect to achieve any general good we too must pursue such a course. If we must have a convention, it must (to be useful) be made up of the friends of certain measures. It is perfectly natural that there should be a diversity of opinion amongst us as to the best means of promoting our improvement, and it would not only be unwise, but it would be perfect madness to bring those discordant opinions together in order that their reciprocal force might paralyze each other, and neither be the means of effecting invaluable results. I believe that the warmest of advocates of a "general convention of the people of color" are to be found amongst those that are very solicitous for our advancement, but who for the most part have had but little experience in public affairs. I have written with much haste, and if this communication will be the means of arousing the attention of the advocates of a convention, so as to define its limits, my object will be attained. I may refer to this subject again provided, someone one offers stronger arguments in favor of an opposite measure. Therefore, if the convention should be held, the limitations that I should suggest would be that it ought to be composed of those friendly to our improvement in this "our home our native land." May 27th, 1840 AMERICANUS.

THE CONVENTION. We have written one or two editorials recently, under a caption, " A Convention," upon the propriety of one and showing some reasons for it, and several communications have also appeared in our columns, within the same one, under the same caption, favorable to the subject, [unclear] of which has confined itself to anything definite as the character and object of the convention, whether to [unclear][/unclear] State or a National one, or as to time or place, excepting [unclear][/unclear] the report of the county convention, held in Jamaica, [unclear][/unclear] [unclear], made up of a few of our respectable brethren from a [unclear][/unclear] towns adjacent to the above place, and even that, while it fixed upon time and place, and suggested some object, set on foot no other measures to be followed up [unclear][/unclear]wards any arrangements, or for convening said convention. We disagreed with our brethren both as to time and [unclear], and for many reasons suggested the propriety of altering both; ; consequently, after waiting a reasonable time, and [unclear] the increasing interest taken by our brethren in different parts of the State, to have a State convention, [unclear][/unclear] called a meeting on Friday evening of last week, when the subject was taken up, the time and place discussed, and well weighed on all sides, and definitely fixed upon, a committee appointed to issue a call, which will be found [unclear], and to carry the subject fully into effect ;-since [unclear], which, we have received a document from Troy, the proceedings of a meeting held there, for the same object, and the place fixed upon the same, as by the meeting hold here, but the time differing.

Now, as the matter is fixed upon to have a State Convention, the time and place also settled, a call prepared, and measures set on foot towards convening the same, and the whole no longer an uncertainty, we have headed this article, THE CONVENTION. With regard to differing with our brethren in Jamaica, both as to time and place, and with our friends in Troy as to time, they must not charge us with a disposition to rule - we were actuated by other and better motives. As to the time, we wished to avoid the height of harvest, as to the harvest, as would be the first of August, to give our honest farming brethren, whom we wish to see present, the opportunity to come; ; this they could not so well have, just at that season of the year. On the other hand, we wished to avoid bringing it too near the State of Anti Slavery meeting, to be held in Utica near that time, but more especially to accommodate another class of our brethren, whom we also wish to be present, but who will probably enter upon also wish to be present, but who will probably their upon duties as teachers, on the first Monday in September. As to the place, we preferred to have it as far West as Utica, but our brethren all disagreed with us, and as to Troy, the place fixed upon by the Jamaica Convention, the friends at Troy thought it best for some reasons, that it should be at Albany, so that the above place is the one fixed upon to hold the Convention. In view of all those fixed upon circumstances, we can but hope that the friends will be generally satisfied with the arrangements, and that we shall consequently have a great gathering of our brethren at the Convention.

Secretary, the following resolutions were unanimously adopted: : Resolved, That a convention of the colored people of the State of New York be invited to assemble at Albany on the 3d Tuesday in August, to consider their political condition, and to adopt such measures as we will be calculated to remove their disabilities, and that a call be issued to that effect. Resolved, That Charles B. Ray, John J. Zuille, Theo. S. Wright, Charles L. A CALL For a CONVENTION OF the colored inhabitants of the state of New York. Fellow Citizens,--We We issue this call, to invite you to attend a State Convention, to be held in the city of Albany on Tuesday, the 18th day of August next. The primary object we have in inviting you to assemble is, to take into consideration the political condition of our people in this State, and to adopt such measures as can be simultaneously carried out by our brethren, in every section of the State, to obtain a relief from those political disabilities under which we labor. . The principal legal disability which affects us is, , our deprivation of the free exercise, in common with other men, of the elective franchise. A free suffrage is the basis of a free government, the would safe-guard of a free people, the strength of the strong, the defence of the weak, a powerful auxiliary to respectability, wealth and usefulness;--and and just in proportion as men are deprived of this, they are shorn of their strength, and are subject to poverty, disgrace, and abuse. We are convinced, fellow citizens, that not only our political, but our was depressed condition in all all other respects in the State, owes itself, not in the least sense, to the fact that we are politically weak, not possessing the unrestricted realistic use of the elective [unclear]fanchied[/unclear]. in The body politic see in us, therefore, no favors to court, and nothing to 'ear. It is to them a matter of no concern, what may be the abuses we suffer, or how unhappy our condition. You are aware, that while other citizens have a free and

[PAGE 7]
unrestricted use of the elective franchise, a property qualification is required on our part, in order for us to exercise this right, so important to a free people, and without which, a man cannot be considered, in a democratic sense, a freeman. This invidious requisition to the exercise of a birth-right privilege, weakens our standing as citizens of the State, and subjects us to all consequent inconvenience. It also degrades our population, because it virtually lowers us in the scale of humanity, and reflects disparagingly upon our character. To seek a removal of this radical evil, is the object of calling you together in convention. There has been no time so favorable for us to meet for the above object, as the present season. There is evidently a redeeming spirit abroad in our State--an increasing disposition to stand by, and defend the weak against the strong, as the noble acts of the Legislature regarding our protection as citizens, clearly indicate. Ought we not, then, to avail ourselves of this favorable indication, and come together to take some decisive measures to lay before the next Legislature our grievances, with a view to produce further action on their part, for our political disenthralment?

[PAGE 8]
To facilitate the business of the convention, it will be necessary that statements selling forth the legal and other disabilities of our people in different parts of the State, be presented at the Convention. To further this object, we invite all who expect to be present, to collect such statements, and also statistical accounts of the property, real and personal, public buildings, with their value, [unclear]& .,[/unclear] owned by our people, and the condition of the people in morals, as compared with former times. We therefore urge upon colored men in all sections of the State-men men in all circumstances-if if you possess self-respect-if if you love liberty-if if you appreciate your own rights-if the prosperity of our people- if you wish for political and moral elevation—if of our - if you have any interest in the prosperity of our people-if if you have any regard for the welfare of your children—for - for the welfare of the State and of the nation, to assemble at Albany on the 18th of August next.

[PAGE 9]
We call upon the farmer to leave for a while his harvesting, and repair to the assemblage of his brethren. Let the mechanic leave his workshop, to share the toils of a general council. Let the laborer and the working man be seen crowding into the avenues that lead to the place of assemblage. Let every portion of our great and growing State, where lives a single object of oppression, be represented. We call upon the people in every city, town and village to represent themselves in that Convention. Let the aged and the youth-all-all-be - all -all - be found at the above place, on that day. Come up, fellow citizens, from Suffolk to Erie, from [unclear]Clinton[/unclear] to Steuben, and let us engage together in a common interest.

For the Colored American. GREAT MEETING AT TROY, N. Y. At a large and spirited meeting of the colored citizens of Troy, held in the Liberty street Presbyterian church, on the evening of May 26th, Mr. Daniel Jones took the chair, and Mr. John H. Smothers was chosen Vice President. H. H. Garnett was appointed Secretary. The meeting was opened by prayer. The object of the meeting having been stated, the following resolutions were presented by the Secretary, and unanimously adopted : Resolved, That with our brethren of Jamaica and New York city, we believe it is expedient and highly important that a State Convention should be held of the colored citizens of New York. Resolved, That the times in which we live, and the duty that we owe to God and man, together with the claims which the rising generation have upon us, call upon every colored man to use his unceasing exertions to remove the various disabilities under which we labor. Resolved, That the chairman appoint a committee of five to prepare an address embodying the views of the this meeting, in regard to a State Convention. Whereupon, the following gentlemen were appointed--William -- William Rich, Alexander Thenay, Jacob Brown, Wm. m S. Baltimore, and H. H. Garnett. The committee, after having withdrawn, returned and presented the following address, which was unanimously adopted. [The address will appear next week.] The meeting was then addressed by Geo. R. Moreton, Marshall Jones, Wm. m Rich, and H. H. Garnett. The following resolution was adopted: Resolved, That the "Colored American," edited and published in New York by Charles B. Ray, shall have our support as long as it continues to be the zealous advocate of the bleeding slave, and the rights of the nominally free. Mr. Wm. P. Johnson, General Agent of the above mentioned paper, being present, was introduced to the meeting, and was listened to with intense interest. On motion, it was Resolved, That the minutes of this meeting to be published in the Colored American. The meeting was then adjourned. H. H. GARNETT, Secretary. May 26th, 1840.
