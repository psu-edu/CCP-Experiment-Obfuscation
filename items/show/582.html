TRANSACTIONS OF THE FIRST ANNUAL MEETING OF THE COLORED TEACHERS' ASSOCIATION, HELD AT SPRINGFIELD, OHIO, DEC. 25 & 26, 1861. MINUTES.-- The members of the Association met in the Church at 2 o'clock, P.M. The President, Jas. S. Waring, called the house to order. Prayer was offered by Mr. Lewis Adams. On motion, G.W. W. Guy was chosen Secretary pro. tem. The Constitution was read, and the names of the following persons were enrolled: J. D. Betts, Mrs, F. M. Mitchell, Miss L. Satchell. On the motion, the Association adjourned, to meet at 7 o'clock, P.M. M. EVENING SESSION. The Association having convened, the deliberations were opened with singing, after which B. K. Sampson delivered the annual address, which was most effective, and worthy the occasion of our coming together. A very excellent essay, written by Mr. H. Bushnell, was then read by the Secretary. Mr. T. J. Ferguson, of the Lebanon, next addressed the Association on the subject of Domestic Culture. On the motion, the names of the following persons were received and enrolled: Miss Susan Adams, Solomon Day, S. Peterson. A collection for defraying expenses was taken up, and the house adjourned to meet at 9 o'clock on Thursday morning.

CONSTITUTION &C. ---***--- ARTICLE I. This Association shall be styled "The Ohio Colored Teachers' Association." ART. II. The Officers of this Association shall be, a President, five Vice Presidents, Recording Secretary, Corresponding Secretary, and Treasurer; all of whom shall constitute the Board of Directors. ART. III. It shall be the duty of the President to preside at all meetings of the Association. In the absence of the President, it shall be the duty of the Vice Presidents to fill the President's office. ART IV. It shall be the duty of the Recording Secretary to keep a correct record of all the proceedings of the Association. ART. V. It shall be the duty of the Corresponding Secretary to communicate, under the direction of the Board of Directors, with similar Associations, should there be any, with the teachers of the state, and with such other persons as may be necessary, He shall keep a full record of all communications, and report, when called upon at any regular meeting of the Association. ART. V. It shall be the duty of the Treasurer to re-ceive and keep all the moneys of the Association - pay out the same only on orders from the Board of Directors; and report the condition of the finances, when called upon at any regular meeting of the Association. ART. VII. It shall be the duty of the Board of Directors to have in charge the general interests of the Association, to make arrangements for its meetings, and to do all in their power to make it a useful and permanent institution. ART. VIII. Any person who is engaged in teaching private or public schools, giving lessons in elementary branches, or engaged in conducting high schools, is eligible to membership. ART. IX. All persons wishing to become members of said Association, shall apply to the Board of Directors, and, being recommended by either of them, shall be re-

3 PROCEEDINGS OF THE ADDRESS to the Patrons and Friends of Colored Schools. FRIENDS AND PATRONS OF OUR SCHOOLS: -- In the language of necessity we address you. Through a generous provision of our state government, , schools are being established throughout the state; and the colored schools are not behind in the process of multiplication. Though they are not as prolific of good as the white schools, this, nevertheless, is attributable in part to matters beyond our control; but the cause which mostly obstructs the progress and efficiency of our schools is in our own hands. The day has passed for man to be used as a mere machine. He must reflect the light of his own moral and intellectual development - must either shine in the effulgence of his own wisdom, or sink to poverty and wretchedness by his own ignorance. For the last ten years, colored teachers have been actively engaged in preparing themselves for the responsible duties of the schoolroom. Many have entered our colleges, and other institutions of learning, and have devoted their time and money for qualifying themselves to become the instructors of your sons and daughters. In many instances they are teaching for less wages than laborers are employed for, who never spent a dime in fitting themselves for the places they fill. But it is not this to which we wish to call to your attention. We are willing to live upon the fare of the humblest, if by doing so doing we can accomplish the object of our desire, namely, the education and elevation of our race. In many instances you do not visit the schools, nor take any interest in them. Your children are allowed to spend too much of their time in idleness and vice. Too often is it the case that reports from various school districts are ladened with accounts of very poor attendance on the part of the pupils. Now why is this, unless it is that parents are too little concerned in regard to the mental improvement of their children? This lamentable and growing evil can only be remedied by the colored people themselves. And it is to them that we now appeal, in behalf of the sleeping faculties of our race. We call on them to

[PAGE 10]

It leads to the avenues of wealth and power and greatness. Then let us not be indifferent to our dearest and best interests, but let us rather profit by the lessons we have learned. Unless we subdue our prejudices, our very efforts for intellectual attainments will be without a marked distinction. Every denominational prejudice helps to retard the progress and destroy the efficiency of schools. Many persons, too, ignorant of the offices of a Holy Religion, have turned them into political contests. Ay, teachers have gone into the schoolroom with all their denominational dogmas, teaching not so much those principles which would tend to the full development of man's intellectual and moral faculties, but rather propagating certain doctrines which can be regarded nothing more than the fixed notions of their own prejudices. This is a fact which we very much regret; and these are obstacles with which we have to contend. We ask your cooperation to the extent of your ability, in the field of labor. Recent accounts show that there are in this state nearly 14,000 colored youth, between 5 and 21 years of age. Of this number, about 7000 have been enrolled in the schools during the year. The daily average attendance is not more than 3,500. The number of colored schools is known to be 160. The number of colored teachers is about 150. In the light of these considerations, the condition of our people calls on you to educate them. The voice of unborn generations calls out to you from the bosom of the future; and the duty which you owe to your children enjoins it upon you. It is a question with many to understand why we should spend so much time and money in educating our children, when it is known they are not allowed to fill the places of lawyers, senators, judges or presidents. We verily admit all this; but does this truth afford the slightest reason that we we should refuse to cultivate our faculties, refuse to assimilate ourselves to the Divine Archetype, and become the representatives of His infinite and perfect wisdom? ? - It is not, rather, necessary that the laboring man, the farmer, the mechanic, hostler, kitchen-girl, should all have a good education? The practical utility of a scientific knowledge is realized in every industrial branch of life. – There is not an individual, however obscure, or whatever

Labor, combined with intelligence, is tenfold more productive than labor expended without forethought. Never can we become an influential or enlightened people in these United States, Canada, Liberia, Hayti, or any other country, unless we educate ourselves. Parents, teach your children that it is not disgraceful to labor. Give them a good education, and they will exert an influence which will be felt in the world. Every age and generation have their peculiar wants. Every year brings us nearer the golden era, when we shall exult in the glory of our mental acquisitions, or discover to ourselves the painful truth of being insufficient for the work before us. The truth of being insufficient for the work before us. The truth of this will become more mortifying to us when we reflect that everything is receiving the impress of improvement. No matter how circumscribed by adverse circumstances, no matter how oppressed by inexorable law; if an opportunity is afforded, we must educate. In surveying the social condition of man, we will find that nothing causes him to feel more self-abased than when he contemplates the dread reality of his own mental imbecility. But ah! ! what joy must animate the breasts of those parents who know that the pledges of their affection have been trained in the school of learning! The best legacy which a parent can leave to a child is a liberal education: without it, youth and manhood float on a sea filled with rocks, and no beacon-light of light to direct them. It is the guiding star which shines in the sky of man's life, which glitters on the brow of night, and points him to the heaven of safety. It is the stepping-stone to wealth and power. It guards against all indisposition, and teaches the method of preserving health. We are fast advancing to a new period in the world's drama. If we fail to meet the wants of the great hereafter, the trials of coming life will be tenfold greater than they are at the present time. And if, in the midst of our multiplied opportunities, the moments are left unimproved, then greater will be our condemnation. Even without an education, you can not reasonably expect your children to succeed as well as you yourselves have done in the daily avocations of life. It is as impossible with them as it wo'd be to stay the flight

You may grow languid over the sad condition of Society; you may even wish to exempt yourselves from the important claims which it has upon you; but so long as society remains in its present state, so long as we linger in comparative darkness, so long will this claim remain the inevitable sequence of our ignorance. Let us, then, acknowledge the claim, and mutually strive to meet it. Let the work of human education be made more efficient, until society shall brighten by our acquisitions, and a light of intellectual glory pervade the earth. J on Address: John G. Mitchell, Tho's J. PROCEEDINGS OF THE SECOND ANNUAL MEETING OF THE OHIO COLORED TEACHERS' ASSOCIATION. HELD IN CINCINNATI, O., DEC. 25, 26 AND 27, 1862 Pursuant to adjournment, the members of the Association met in the hall of the school house in the Eastern District, at 2 o'clock, P.M. M. The President, Mr. J. S. Waring, called the house to order, made a few appropriate remarks, and invited the officers elect to take their places. Mr. J. G. Mitchell then took the chair. T. J. Ferguson, Recording Secretary, read the Constitution. S. D. Fox, J. M. Meek, A. N. Freeman and Miss B. V. Harris, being recommended, were received full members of the Association. The following Teachers then reported verbally concerning the condition of their schools: G. W. Guy, T. J. Ferguson, J. S. Waring, J. R. Blackburn, Miss S. J. Woodson, F. A. Trotter, B. V. Harris, Mr. J. M. Meek, S. D. Fox, A. N. Freeman and J. G. Mitchell. On motion, it was resolved that the evening meetings commence at half past 7 o'clock. Mr. T. J. Furguson moved that the day sessions commence at 9 o'clock, A. M., and 2 o'clock, P. M., and that each session continue three hours. Carried. G. W. Guy was appointed Treasurer pro tem. The Association adjourned, to meet at half past 7 o'clock, in Baker street Church.

The Association convened at half past 7 o'clock. Singing by the choir. After which, prayer by the Rev. S. D. Fox. Miss S. J. Woodson, a graduate of Oberlin College, delivered an address to the youth. Miss Woodson is a fine speaker and a young lady of rare talents. Her address was replete with arguments ingenious and subtle, thoughts noble and aspiring. Music by the choir. Mrs. S. J. Hunster, of Xenia, read an essay. Subject, Harmony; Religious or Practical. An excellent essay was read by S. J. Meek. Subject, Sin of Insubordination. Music. An essay by Miss F. A. Trotter. Subject, A Solid or Genuine Education. It was clearly set forth in this essay that such an education does not make a man an egotist, a fop or a braggadocio, but enables him to move among his fellow men with meekness, honor and dignity. Mr. B. K. Sampson next addressed the audience. The theme of his discourse was Education the Price of Liberty. Mr. Sampson is a very eloquent speaker. In argument, he is ingenious; in delivery, earnest and bold; in persuasion, mild and winning. Music by the choir. The Association then adjourned.

It was argued by some that the colored man has proved himself to be a man on nearly every battle-field of the American Revolution, and that in other places of daring, honor and trust, he has set forth the nobleness of his manhood; ; by some again that the colored man was a man, and that the discussion of such a subject exhibited weakness or ignorance on the part of the disputants. It was argued by others that the colored man could become a man by cultivating and fully developing the powers and susceptibilities of the mind, by cultivating a great, good and benevolent heart, by accumulating wealth. That the colored man must act now, and continue to act; that what he has done in the forgetful past will have but little weight in comparison with what he is now doing. In reply to the objection urged against the topic, it was remarked that the word man, in the topic under discussion, was used in contradistinction to the word man, in its generic sense, that it was used to include whatever was great, and good and noble, and also had reference to our social and political equality. On motion, the following resolutions were adopted: : WHEREAS, The good people of the Cincinnati have shown their interest in the cause of education by sharing with us their homes and their comforts during our stay among them; therefore, be it Resolved, That our heartfelt thanks are due to them for their kind hospitality. Resolved, That our thanks are hereby tendered to the Trustees of the Colored Common Schools, and to the Trustees of Baker street Church, for the use of their buildings. Resolved, That our thanks are equally due the Choir, for cheering our hearts with sweet music while engaged in the arduous duties of the Association. Constitution. Preamble. - To diffuse a knowledge of the art of teaching, and to promote and advance the cause of popular education, we, whose names are subjoined, do adopt the following Constitution: : Article I. This Association shall be styled " "The Ohio Colored Teachers' Association." Art. II. The officers of the Association shall be, a President, five Vice-Presidents, Recording Secretary, Corresponding Secretary and Treasurer; ; all of whom shall constitute the Board of Directors. Art. III. . It shall be the duty of the President to preside at all meetings of the Association. In the absence of the President, it shall be the duty of one of the Vice-Presidents to fill the President's office. Art. IV. It shall be the duty of the Recording Secretary to keep a correct record of all the proceedings of the Association. Art. V. It shall be the duty of the Corresponding Secretary to communicate, under the direction of the Board of Directors, with similar Associations, should there be any, with similar Associations, should there be any, with the teachers of the State, and with such other persons as may be necessary. He shall keep a full record of all communications, and report, when called upon at any regular meeting of the Association. Art. VI. It shall be the duty of the Treasurer to receive and keep all the moneys of the Association; ; pay out the same only on orders from the Board of Directors, and report the condition of the finances, when called upon at any regular meeting of the Association. Art. VII. It shall be the duty of the Board of Directors to have in charge the general interests of the Association, to make arrangements for its meetings, and to do all in their power to make it a useful and permanent institution.

12 CONSTITUTION ART. VIII. Any person who is engaged in teaching private or public schools, giving lessons in elementary branches, , or engaged in conducting high schools, is eligible to membership. All persons wishing to become members of said Association, shall apply to the Board of Directors, and, being recommended by either of them, shall be received full members by a vote of two-thirds of the members present, on paying fifty cents for males, and twenty-five five cents for females.
