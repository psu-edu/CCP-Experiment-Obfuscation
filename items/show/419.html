CLEVELAND DAILY HERALD. SATURDAY EVENING, AUGUST 26, 1843. PUBLISHED DAILY, IN THE CENTRAL BUILDINGS, BY J. A. HARRIS. [unclear]Taxes[/unclear] - Daily Paper eight dollars per annum, payable half yearly. Country Paper two dollars per annum, payable in advance. All new advertisements appear in both papers. NATIONAL CONVENTION OF COLORED MEN - NEGRO COLONY IN OHIO. Every effort of the colored race in this country to better their condition and elevate themselves in the estimation & regard of the whites, must meet the approval and encouragement of the liberal and the philanthropic. Cut off by laws, many of them unjust and oppressive, from nearly every right and privilege of citizenship, the down-trodden colored race continues to increase and multiply among us, and in spite of the difficulties and embarrassments under which they labor are gradually, as a whole, improving in pecuniary, mental, social, and moral condition. While the red men fade away like the mist of the morning as the march of civilization progresses, the negro not only maintains his ground but advances with increased strength in the great current of population that fills the valleys and peoples the mountain tops.

[PAGE 2]
A National Convention of Colored Men was held at Buffalo recently, which continued in session for several days. All the non-slave-holding States, and some of the others, were represented by 75 delegates. The object of the Convention was to devise means to elevate the condition of their race, and the Gazelle says the proceedings were every way creditable, and exhibited much talent. Mr. Beman, of Philadelphia presided, and Messrs. Gerneit[/unclear], of Troy, C. L. Raymond, of Salem, Mass., Douglass, of Conn., and Wright, of New York, were the principal speakers, and are all, except Mr. Douglass, graduates of some of the literary institutions of the country. Mr. Douglass was formerly a slave. The Gazette says: On Wednesday afternoon we listened to a speech from Mr. Garnett, which, for bold, genuine, soul stirring eloquence, we scarce ever heard equalled. It fairly enchained the audience, over whose passions, affections and actions, the speaker appeared to have perfect control. Mr. Raymond, too, is an eloquent speaker, and a man of general and diversified information.

[PAGE 3]
An address to the colored people was adopted -- -making a stirring appeal to them for action in the great cause of self-elevation and self- self-advancement, and shewing that all other causes of their debasement may be removed except that of color. A corresponding committee of two from each State was appointed, with power to call future conventions, &c, and resolutions were adopted for the circulation of petitions to Congress for the abolition of slavery in the District of Columbia and in the Territories, and against the annexation of Texas, &c. &c.

[PAGE 4]
A Convention of the colored people of Ohio was called sometime since, to be held at Columbus on the 10th of August. We have seen no report of the proceedings. The objects of the Convention were the general advancement of the interests of the race, and for adopting measures to obtain their rights as citizens of a free state. A manifesto has recently been published by a [unclear] colony of their people at Carthagena, in Mercer county, in reply to an invitation extended to them to send a representation to the proposed convention. The meeting, after considering the invitation, resolved to decline, and appointed a committee to draft a suitable statement of their reasons therefor, an extract from which we subjoin, and will be read we think with deep interest. It shows that the negro race are capable of better things than they now exhibit, and to give practical proof of this it is only necessary for them to have scope for their industry, enterprise and intelligence.

[PAGE 5]
The colonists at Carthagena, having abandoned the menial occupations "which popular opinion assigns to colored people," and "resolved likewise to do something for themselves," they say in their circular that they have succeeded to their best wishes, and are for the presented contended. They have acquired large possessions, have built comfortable dwellings, cultivate good farms, possess cattle and sheep, and manufacture their own clothing &c. They state they have encountered no hostility or ill-treatment from their neighbors, but on the contrary they "attend our meetings" says the circular, "come to our mills, employ our mechanics and day-laborers, buy our provisions, and we do the same by them. That is, we all seek our interest and convenience without regard to color." We add the concluding portion of the circular, which contains many useful suggestions.

[PAGE 6]
We have meeting houses, and a school house. We have had a good school most of the time for six years. Our children have learned to read, write, and cypher. We have Sunday schools, where they are taught the principles of morality and religion. We have a saw-mill and a grist-mill. We are striving to live a quiet and orderly life. We wish to have our character plead for us. We wish to have our property stand out and ask for the protection of the law. We wish to have those who oppose us, witness our quiet and industry; that they may be satisfied of our character, not by what we say but by what we do. Our voice has been heard but once at Columbus, by the legislature asking a repeal of the "black laws." And we may not now send a delegate to your convention; not because we disapprove of the object, but because we believe there is a more excellent way. Seven years ago when our settlement first began, there was the common prejudice against us, that we should not be able to take care of ourselves; and of course, what we lacked in the supplying of our wants, they thought must come off from them.

[PAGE 7]
But that we are not idlers is now apparent from the fact of our having cleared up 1000 acres of wild land; made and laid up 50,000 rails, and built at least 200 different kinds of buildings, (to say nothing of some $ 10,000 which individuals of us have paid for our freedom,) besides having in our settlement a [unclear]hatter[/unclear], a wagon maker, a blacksmith, a tanner, a shoemaker, carpenters, masons, weavers, most of whom find constant employment. We have also built several brick kilns. The means by which we obtain a livelihood, are seen and known by all our neighbors; and the result is as we have stated. We have receive no more damage from our white neighbors than we do from one another. And now, our colored brethren, we appeal to you, especially to those of you who live in towns and follow those precarious occupations for a livelihood, which prejudice has assigned to you, would you not be serving your country

[PAGE 8]
and your race, to more purpose, if you were to leave your present residences and employments, and go into the country and become a part of the bone and sinew of the land. Now, in time of peace let us be useful in carrying on agriculture, and commerce and the mechanical arts. We shall eventually be valued according to our worth. Before the legislature will make laws to protect us, one of two things must take place.-- [insertion]--[/insertion] Either the white people must have a revival of faith in their own principles of "liberty," and make equal laws for the love of justice, without regard to what they consider bad character: : or we, the colored people, must become more valuable to the state. We must help it to raise a revenue, and increase its wealth by throwing our labor into profitable employments.

[PAGE 9]
We must not be a nuisance, but we must seem not to be so. Our employments must be of that character that people can see how we obtain our livelihood, and that we are useful. What is it to the state when a waiter, or a boot-black, or a cook dies? What profit to the state is all their labor? If we, then as a people, are a useless class, and can show no fruit as the result of their industry, why should we not be suspected, despised and ridiculed? But, on the other hand, if our labor is all honorable and profitable, both to ourselves and the state, we shall have the increased satisfaction of a good living, and a good name, besides something to leave as an inheritance to our children. We see then, brethren, but these two ways of obtaining our rights. The first proposes a change in the oppressor, and the second a change in the oppressed. The one honorable to our oppressors, the other profitable to ourselves.- -- Let us do our duty, and leave the event with Providence. With sentiments of respect, and with feelings of love and good will to our colored brethren, and best wishes for their welfare, we subscribe ourselves your friends and fellow citizens. M. P. Jones, D. F. Hardy, J. Bowles; S. Thomas S. Gree, D. F. Bowles, Geo. [unclear][Oveton], J. Buwles, P. Ward, S. Jones, T. Epsom, S. Gree, Commitee These people have come to the conclusion which will secure the most general approval and result in their own greatest good.
