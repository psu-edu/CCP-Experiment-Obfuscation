The Colored Convention. BUFFALO, August 24th, 1843. The National Convention of colored men, which convened at this place on the 15th, closed its sessions last Saturday. From forty to fifty 'regular' delegates, (of whom nine, at least, were clergymen,) were present. There was also present a large number of citizens of Western New-York, York, and some from the other States, and some from Canada, who, feeling a deep interest in the cause of universal liberty, and supposing that the call for the Convention, for all who could make it convenient to attend, and aid with their wisdom in the deliberations of the meeting,' was broad enough to secure them to them a hearing in the Convention; but, unfortunately, they were without the necessary regular credentials. At 10 o'clock, S.H. H. Davis of Buffalo took the chair, and called the meeting to order. The first business was to determine who should take part in the deliberations of the Convention. It was evident there were those present who had come with the determination to carry certain measures, and to that end, and also to have as little debate as possible, except by themselves, and it was maintained that none should be allowed to speak but 'regular' delegates. In defense of this tyrannical position, this gag rule, it was contended by Rev. H. H. Garnet, Rev. C. Monroe, Rev. S. S. Wright, & &c. that their constituents had sent them to the Convention to do certain business; ; that after all that might be said, it must be done; ; that they could not afford to spend time in listening to speeches from those who were not 'regular' regular ´ delegates; ; besides, the presumption was, that all who felt an interest in the object for which they had assembled, had appointed 'regular' regular´ delegates.

[PAGE 2]
The Massachusetts delegates, and a number of others, entered their protest against such a sectarian, anti-liberty, anti-abolition position. It was in opposition to the practice of all Conventions claiming to be free--in - in opposition to the principles for which we have been contending for years gone by-in opposition to the principles for the furtherance of which this Convention had been called. However, it had been determined by those who claimed to know in what way the Convention would best subserve certain ends, for which they came to the Convention, that none but those with 'regular' papers should speak. The question was called; ; the vote, being a tie, was decided against liberty, by the casting vote of the chair. C. L. Remond declared his intention not to act with the Convention which could adopt such a gag rule, and accordingly withdrew. F. Douglass said he would not leave them, but remain, in hopes that the vote would be reconsidered, and the Convention made a free one. The hour of 12 having arrived, the Convention took recess. Met at 2 P.M. M. After prayer by one of the divines, proceeded to business. The Committee previously appointed, reported a list of officers for the Convention, who were severally voted in. A reconsideration of the vote of the forenoon was now moved, and a debate ensued, which consumed the whole of the afternoon, so determined was the opposition of some to a free Convention. F. Douglass

[PAGE 3]
contended nobly for freedom, in opposition to Rev. C. Monroe and others. It was found that the exclusive principle would not go, and a resolution was introduced, allowing all persons from those parts of the United States, (not Canada, as that would interfere with certain action intended to be had by the Convention in favor of a certain party, whose jurisdiction extends not to Canada,) from which there are no 'regular' delegates, to sit as 'regular' delegates, and all others allowed the privilege of sitting as 'honorary' members. This resolution passed by a large majority, who supposed that all was granted by it which could be asked. Upon its passage, C. L. Remond took his seat as a member of the Convention. The hour of 6 having arrived, the Convention adjourned. The first business before the Convention next morning, was a set of resolutions relating to the church and ministry. They spoke out plainly and truthfully. They were opposed by some of the clerical delegates, on the ground that, although they were true, yet it was duty to remain in the church to exert a christian influence over her! They passed, however. Then came a resolution endorsing for 'Liberty Party,' and making it the way in which slavery is to be abolished. It was advocated by Rev. H. H. Garnet, Rev. C. Monroe, Rev. C. B. Ray, and Rev. S. S. Wright; opposed by C. L. Remond, F. Douglass, W. W. Brown, and others. It was contended that this Convention, professing to represent the colored people, had no right to commit itself to any sect or party. During the debate, Wm. L. Garrison was brought in for his share of abuse. A parallel was run between him and G. J. G. Birney, in which an attempt was made to show that the sacrifices made by the latter left the former entirely in the shade. The Rev. gentlemen were replied to by Douglass and Remond, to the satisfaction of the people, if not exactly to their own satisfaction. It was found, however, on taking the vote, that that in getting up the Convention they had not labored in vain, for a sufficient number were true (or false if you please) to pass the resolution. In the afternoon, Rev. H. H. Garnet introduced his address to the slaves, which contained, among other things, advice to this effect: that the slave was to go to his master, tell of the injustice of slavery, the duty of immediate emancipation--to - to refuse to work another hour, unless paid; and if his master remained inexorable, 'then to strike for liberty.' In his speech in favor of the address, he affirmed ' 'that the time had come to resort to this course; that other means had failed, and would fail; that abolitionists, who, by the bye, were very benevolent men, had done about all that they could do; that non-resistance was ridiculous, and not to be thought of, even for the present, by the slaves. The Rev. gentleman grew eloquent; the axioms of Patrick Henry and others were pressed into service on the occasion--'Give - 'Give me liberty, or give me death.' 'Resistance to tyrants is obedience to God.' The time has come. He was ready for 'war to the knife, and knife to the hilt.' In his flights of fancy he seemed rather addressing a victorious army, than a gang of crushed and imbruted slaves, despoiled of all rights, and without the means of successful resistance. He did not seem to forget, however, that Mason and Dixon's line was some hundreds of miles south of

[PAGE 4]
him and his family. True to the interests of the slave, the Massachusetts delegates, on this as on every other occasion, (where the previous question was not resorted to to cut off debate,) were at their post, and ready to defend the right. The sophistry of the Rev. gentleman, and his unwillingness to meet the question on its merits, was shown up. The light shed-the good already accomplished-the example afforded in the abolition of West India slavery, without bloodshed-the the change in public opinion-enemies giving way-and, in view of these facts, the responsibility involved in putting forth such a document, were ably and effectually presented to the Convention. The address was recommitted, but came back about the same thing. Nearly or quite another day was consumed on it. The question on its adoption was called for, without the least apparent doubt on the part of its friends, that it would pass. When the name of C.B. B. Ray was called, he declined voting, on the ground that he had been so busy during the debates, as not to know whether to vote for it or not. On polling the vote it was found that it was lost by one vote. Ray now requested to be allowed to vote, having another reason to offer, it, to wit, when the vote was taken he had no doubt that it would be in favor of the address, or else he would have voted in favor of it. Being called on by Mr. Remond to act like a man and not attempt to sit on two stools, he concluded not to press his request. In the evening, Rev. C. Monroe presented a resolution declaring that 'by the second section of the fourth article of the Constitution of the U.S., we were citizens the object of which was, to convert the Constitution into an anti-slavery document; ; upon this he made a flaming speech, but it would not do. Facts were better than logic, even had there been any of that article in his speech. A reconsideration of the vote on the address to the slaves was then moved, as the intermission had afforded time to get some of the 'bolters' into the traces again, and as three who had voted against the address had gone home, it was thought that the Convention could be made to endorse for it. The chairman, A.G. G. Beman, of New-Haven, then took the floor, remarking that he wished to speak at least an hour; ; the rule confining speakers to fifteen minutes was suspended, and a very able speech was made by him. He was followed by Garnet, who occupied more than an hour in making inflammatory appeals to the feelings of the Convention. He

[PAGE 5]
was followed by Remond, who spoke some twenty-five minutes, during which, several attempts were made by the few in favor of the address to gag him down, by calling the previous question. At the close of Remond's remarks, considerable confusion ensued, growing out of attempts of different individuals to get the floor; the spectators began to disperse; the Convention in tumult, in the midst of which, the hour of twelve, Friday night, which had previously been fixed on by the Convention as the hour of final adjournment, arrived. The chairman announced the fact, and declared the Convention dissolved by its own vote. Garnet said if the Convention was dissolved, he would hold the chairman responsible. After some minutes spent in seeing who could talk the loudest, the chairman succeeded in getting order; it was then moved to adjourn until the next morning at 9 o'clock; a number of the Convention then left, among them Remond and Douglass. It was then proposed to turn out all who were not members of the Convention, which was accordingly done, and the doors closed. The Convention, or the part of it then present, was then called to order, and the address again called up. Garnet and Ray were understood to be in favor of voting on it then! That was objected to; half-past 7 next morning was then urged by Ray. It was replied, that the Convention stood adjourned to 9 o'clock next morning, and that good faith would be expected by the citizens. Garnet was understood to say that the citizens need not know what they did; they had other business to do at 9 o'clock, &c. It would not do, however, and they finally concluded to meet according to adjournment, and at half-past 9, take the vote on the address, without debate. Convention met accordingly, on Saturday morning, and on counting the vote on the address, it was found there were but nine in favor of it. Garnet then gave notice that he should publish the address on his own responsibility, with the names of those who are willing to sign it. The Convention then adjourned to meet at Troy, N.Y., on the call of the Committee which had been previously appointed for that purpose. Yours for the night, E. A. Marsh
