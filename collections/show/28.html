Title

1848 National Convention of Colored Freemen held in Cleveland OH

Description

The 1848 National Convention of Colored Freemen was held in Cleveland, Ohio, from Wednesday, September 6, to Friday, September 8. The city’s courthouse hosted nationally renowned Black leaders, including Frederick Douglass and Martin R. Delany, alongside prominent Ohio leaders. This convention marked the first national gathering of its kind ever hosted in the Midwest. Cleveland’s decision to allow the delegates to use the courthouse — with the permission of the Cleveland Bar Association — demonstrated the city’s effort to attract Black residents for its economic growth.

This geographical context holds particular significance, as free African Americans were actively seeking opportunities in the western, relatively new states, drawn by industrial and educational prospects despite encountering pervasive anti-Black racism. Hosting a national Colored Convention in Cleveland not only reflected the growing appeal of western cities to African Americans but also underscored the emergence of a mature Black community in the Midwest. The 1848 convention also marked a generational shift in leadership. While earlier national conventions were primarily led by New York-based figures such as Henry Highland Garnet, James McCune Smith, and Alexander Crummell, the 1848 event showcased a younger generation of Black leaders, including Douglass, Delany, William Howard Day, and the Langston brothers.

These changes in leadership brought diverse and, at times, conflicting opinions on issues such as education, abolition, women’s rights, and emigration. For instance, the convention revealed tensions between the Black working class and the Black elite. Leaders like Delany advocated for achieving respectability through education, while Black Ohioans — many of whom had settled in newly established urban areas with their manual skills — resisted the prejudice against working-class occupations. Similarly, the convention addressed women’s rights but excluded Black women from leadership roles. Douglass, as the convention’s president, invited Rebecca Sanford, a young white advocate for women’s rights, to deliver a speech. However, her address failed to reflect the realities faced by Black women. While the delegates resolved to include women in their deliberations, their efforts fell short of adequately addressing Black women’s needs. As the 1849 Ohio State Convention demonstrated, Black women, including Jane Merritt, took action against the male-dominated leadership of the Colored Conventions, leading a push for greater representation and inclusion.
