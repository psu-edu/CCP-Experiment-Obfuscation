Title

1837 Convention of Colored People of Ohio held in Columbus

Description

The 1837 Ohio State Convention is the earliest-known state convention. Although a full proceeding has not been found, a New Richmond abolitionist newspaper, The Philanthropist, published two articles about the event. It is likely that the convention committee invited reporters from The Philanthropist and Ohio Political Register to cover the event and later sent them the convention proceedings for publication to highlight this statewide initiative, organized and led by African Americans. While it may be assumed that a variety of topics and issues were discussed, the newspaper coverage focuses primarily on the delegates’ discussions about education and the notorious Black laws.

The Convention of Colored People, held in Columbus, in August 1837, emphasized the educational and social advancement of African Americans in the state. Organized under the leadership of Reverend [William] C. Yancy [or Yancey] and other delegates, the convention sought to establish a “School Fund and Mutual Improvement Institution” to promote the “moral and religious elevation” of Black Ohioans. The delegates resolved to draft a constitution for the institution, appoint a general agent, and seek legislative support for its incorporation. The proposed institution’s constitution outlined its governance structure, including officers, an executive committee, and auxiliary districts led by vice presidents. The institution would be funded through contributions from individuals, legacies, religious groups, and state appropriations. Membership required a nominal fee, and the funds were designed for educational and institutional improvements. 

The convention produced key documents, including a petition to the Ohio legislature. This petition sought financial support for schools and called for the repeal of discriminatory Black laws enacted in 1804 and 1807. These laws restricted the rights of Black Ohioans by, for example, preventing them from providing legal testimony against white individuals and imposing bond requirements for residence in the state. The petition also emphasized the economic contributions of Black Ohioans and advocated for equal treatment under revised, equitable law.
